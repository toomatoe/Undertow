Step 1 of 3 · Read the update

First, what actually happened?

A filing is a document a company sends to the SEC, the US agency that oversees securities markets. A signal in Undertow is a label for an event worth researching.

Fictional filing · Practice only

Example Company plans a new share offering

The company plans to issue additional shares to raise money for its operations.

What is a share?

A share represents a small ownership stake in a company. If more shares are issued, the same number of existing shares represents a smaller percentage of the company.

What does this update tell us?

Open an answer to see the explanation. You can explore all three.

The company plans to issue new shares.

That's right.New shares can raise money for the company and reduce existing shareholders' percentage ownership. That is called dilution. The filing alone does not tell us what the stock price will do.

The stock price will definitely fall.

The direction is still unknown.The update says the company plans to issue shares. More shares can dilute existing ownership, but a filing is not a price forecast.

The company has reported a profit.

An offering is different from a profit announcement.An offering is a plan to raise money by issuing shares. This update does not report a profit.

Step 2 of 3 · Compare fairly

A positive number needs context.

Undertow looks at groups of similar past events. Comparing their average move with a benchmark (a reference for comparison) helps put that move in context.

Hypothetical numbers · Invented for practice

Example group average
+2%
Benchmark over the same period
+3%

These are group averages before costs. Individual companies may lose value. This is not a measured Undertow result or a simulated portfolio return.

How did the example group compare with its benchmark?

Open an answer to check your thinking.

It did better because it rose 2%.

It rose, but the benchmark rose more.Subtract 3% from 2%: the group lagged by 1 percentage point. A positive move is not automatically a better result.

It lagged by 1 percentage point.

That's right: 2% − 3% = −1 percentage point.This is a comparison of averages, not a prediction or a result for every company. Costs would also matter in a simulated portfolio.

Every company in the group rose 2%.

An average combines different outcomes.Some companies can fall even when the average rises. Here the group average also lagged the benchmark by 1 percentage point.

Step 3 of 3 · Know the limits

Evidence is a starting point.

Past results help frame questions. They cannot establish what happens next, or prove that a filing caused a price move. Other news, costs, and missing data can change the picture.

A useful distinction

“What happened before?” is a research question.
“What will happen next?” remains uncertain.

What if there is no evidence beside a signal?

The relevant study may not have been run, the sample may be too small, or the local service may be unavailable. Missing evidence is not a positive or negative finding.

What is a useful next step when reading a live signal?

Open an answer to explore the reasoning.

Assume this company will match the average.

A single company can behave very differently.An average describes a group of past events. Reading the original filing and the study's limits gives the result needed context.

Treat a missing result as proof of no risk.

Missing evidence does not mean no risk.It means we do not have a result to assess. The original filing, sample size, time period, and limitations are useful places to look next.

Read the filing and check how the comparison was made.

That's right.Look at the original source, the number of events, the time period, and the study's limits. A signal invites research; it does not prescribe an action.
